01 / Fundamentals

What is hydrogen as an energy carrier?

Hydrogen (H₂) is the lightest element and the most abundant in the universe. It is not an energy source itself, but an energy carrier — it can be produced, stored, and used to generate energy without CO₂ emissions.

How much hydrogen energy converts to electricity. Gasoline engine: only ~25%. Battery: ~90%.
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O + energy
Hydrogen + oxygen = water + energy (heat or electricity)

How is energy produced?

  • Combustion: Hydrogen combines with oxygen, releases heat — product is clean water
  • Fuel cells: Electrochemical reaction H₂ + O₂ generates electricity directly

Why hydrogen?

  • Zero emissions: The only combustion product is H₂O
  • Storage: Can store renewable energy
  • Versatility: Transport, industry, heating

Hydrogen vs other energy sources — where does it make sense?

Hydrogen is not a universal solution. It has specific niches where it outperforms alternatives. Below is a comparison with main competitors.

✓ Where hydrogen wins
  • Heavy transport — trucks, buses, trains (range + fast refueling)
  • High-temperature industry — steel, cement, glass (batteries can't deliver 1500°C)
  • Renewable energy storage — seasonal storage of excess energy
  • Shipping and aviation — batteries too heavy
✗ Where batteries win
  • Passenger cars — better efficiency (90% vs 50%), cheaper infrastructure
  • Short urban distances — overnight charging sufficient
  • Small devices — phones, laptops, tools
  • Where outlets are available — no hydrogen station needed

Conclusion: Hydrogen and batteries are not competitors, but complements. Batteries for small applications and short routes, hydrogen for industry and heavy transport. Together they enable full decarbonization.

02 / Production

Hydrogen production methods

Why "colors" of hydrogen? Hydrogen itself is colorless. Colors are conventional names describing the production method and associated CO₂ emissions. The "greener" the hydrogen, the smaller the carbon footprint — but also higher cost (for now).

Gray hydrogen
"Traditional" — from natural gas, CO₂ escapes to atmosphere
  • How it works: Natural gas + steam → hydrogen + CO₂ (released)
  • Emissions: 9-10 kg CO₂/kg H₂
  • Cost: €1.5-2.5/kg (cheapest)
  • Share: 95% of current production
  • Problem: High emissions — doesn't solve climate issue
Blue hydrogen
"Transitional" — from gas, but CO₂ is captured and stored
  • How it works: Like gray, but CO₂ goes underground (CCS)
  • Emissions: 1-4 kg CO₂/kg H₂ (60-90% reduction)
  • Cost: €2.5-3.5/kg
  • Share: Growing (pilot projects)
  • Role: Bridge between gray and green (until ~2035)
Green hydrogen
"Target" — from water, renewable electricity, zero emissions
  • How it works: Wind/solar electricity splits water into H₂ and O₂
  • Emissions: <1 kg CO₂/kg H₂ (practically zero)
  • Cost: €3-6/kg (2024) → target €1.5-3/kg by 2030
  • Share: <1% today, but growing fast
  • Future: The only fully sustainable option

What is hydrogen used for in these sectors?

Refineries (42%)
Hydrogen removes sulfur from crude oil and "cracks" heavy fractions into lighter fuels (gasoline, diesel).
Ammonia (37%)
Hydrogen + nitrogen = ammonia (NH₃). The main component of synthetic fertilizers, which feed half of humanity.
Methanol (13%)
Hydrogen + CO₂ = methanol. A chemical feedstock for producing plastics, paints, and adhesives.
Steel (4%)
Hydrogen can replace coke in steel production — producing only water instead of CO₂.

05 / Conclusions

Challenges and key takeaways

Challenges

  • High costs: Green H₂ still 2-3× more expensive than gray
  • Lack of infrastructure: Limited pipeline network and refueling stations
  • End-to-end efficiency: Only 30-40% of energy reaches end user
  • Safety: High flammability, leak risk (small molecule)
  • Scaling electrolysis: Shortage of electrolyzers and green energy

Key takeaways

  • Essential for decarbonization: Only realistic option for heavy industry and transport
  • Investment window 2024-2030: Subsidies, long-term contracts, capacity building
  • "Colors" strategy: Gray → blue → green as transition pathway
  • Scale effects: Costs will fall with renewable and electrolyzer market growth
  • International trade: New H₂ supply chains (Australia, Chile, Middle East → Europe)

Summary: Hydrogen is not a universal solution, but a key element of the energy transition. Success depends on falling renewable costs, infrastructure development, and stable regulatory support. Companies investing in 2024-2030 will gain competitive advantage in the low-carbon economy.
